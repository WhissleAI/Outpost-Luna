Data report · Arc Transcriber Space

Teaching a model to hear the Moon.

Arc Transcriber Space is the speech model built for Outpost Luna. Its core data is a corpus of real NASA speech: Artemis crew audio and NASA's own podcasts, where astronauts, flight controllers and scientists talk about the Moon in their own words. This page covers what is in it, how it is labelled, how test sets are kept out, and what the model measures.

The model on Hugging Face Code: arc_transcriber

Uses NASA open media; not endorsed by NASA. Model weights CC BY-NC 4.0 (see The model, below).

Why this corpus

General speech models stumble on exactly the words Outpost Luna needs most.

  • Real mission speech. Crew and mission control talk over radio loops, with noise, cross-talk and clipped phrasing. Clean read speech has none of this.
  • The Moon's vocabulary. Crater and mare names, landing sites, spacecraft and missions: Tycho, Shackleton, Sinus Medii, Orion, Artemis II. A general model writes "tie co" for Tycho.
  • Real people explaining it. In the podcasts, NASA experts explain the Moon in plain language. That is close to the questions people ask the app.
  • Long context. Whole episodes keep earlier sentences and the names already mentioned, so the model learns to use context: what was just said, and the list of names on screen.

How it is annotated

The transcripts are where the labels start. Every segment gets the labels the model returns at run time.

Spoken-form transcripts
Text is written the way it is said: "apollo twelve", "nineteen sixty nine". This is the same normalization the app's names lists use, so what the model hears and what it links to line up.
Entity tags
Mentions are tagged inline by type: lunar feature, site, celestial body, spacecraft, mission, crew, plus numbers, places and organisations.
Names lists
About 9 in 10 segments carry a names list like the one the app sends: names with their kind, in order of relevance. The model learns to link a mention to the right entry, and to say "not in the list" when it should.
Context
Earlier text from the same episode and the names already mentioned travel with each segment, so the model learns to use context without depending on it.
Sounds and channel
Radio beeps, static, music beds and cross-talk are labelled as events, not dropped. Who is speaking is kept anonymous.

The full rules for names lists are in the names-list guide.

Keeping test sets out

A score means something only if the model never saw the test audio.

  • Test recordings never enter. All 15 recordings of our Artemis test set were skipped when the corpus was loaded, so they are not in the training data at all.
  • Whole episodes, checked against every test set. Each episode's text is searched for every test sentence of 6 or more words. The test sets cover Artemis, Apollo, open read speech, synthetic space questions, held-out names and Common Voice. Any segment that equals a test sentence of 4 or more words also counts as a hit. One hit removes the whole episode. 32 episodes (20.3 h) were removed: 9 Artemis recordings that rebroadcast test audio, and 23 podcast episodes that matched only common phrases, removed anyway to be safe.
  • A new held-out test set from the corpus. About 2.5% of each podcast series is set aside, at least 2 episodes per series, chosen by a fixed hash. Only episodes with less than 5% 8-word overlap with any earlier training text qualify, so no model has seen them. 19 episodes, 12.8 h, 10,410 segments (Houston We Have a Podcast 11, Small Steps, Giant Leaps 5, Curious Universe 3), frozen before training.
  • General speech as a guard. The model is also scored on a fixed sample of the Common Voice English test set. Those test sentences are banned from training, so space training can't quietly cost it everyday speech.

What the model measures

Each set has 300 held-out utterances. Each utterance comes with a names list made of its own names plus 20 similar-sounding decoys. All runs use the int8 model that runs on a laptop CPU.

AI use

Space Apps asks every team to say where AI tools were used. Machine-readable: ai-disclosure.json.

Code
Written with AI coding assistants. Commits made with them say so in a co-author line. The team reviewed and tested every change.
Writing
Lesson text, "Why?" cards and CAPCOM lines were drafted with AI help. Every fact is checked against the NASA source it cites.
Data
Part of the speech models' training data is synthetic: text-to-speech audio and AI-generated variations, such as mispronounced Moon names. Reported results are measured on held-out real clips.
Images
The app icon and the team image were made with an AI image generator. They contain no NASA logos, flags or insignia. App screenshots are real captures; Moon and Earth imagery is NASA's.
Audio
CAPCOM's scripted lines were recorded on a Mac. The optional Steer voice is an AI text-to-speech model. In the launch film, the learner's spoken lines are AI-generated speech.
Not AI
NASA data, NASA video and crew audio, and app screenshots.
